# Career-os should enforce proof, not just label it

Career-os sits in a lane nobody else occupies: the open-source and commercial field has converged on downstream job-search operations (scanning, tailoring, tracking, autofill), while career-os works upstream on identity, evidence and naming for people who fit no standard title. The most useful outside research does not argue for widening that scope; it argues for hardening what career-os already does. Three findings carry most of the weight. First, a 2026 study found LLM resume rewriting produced at least one unsupported claim in **96.7% of outputs**, and prompt guardrails alone still left fabrications in **half** of them, which means career-os's provenance tags need a machine check behind them, not just instructions. Second, hiring-side evidence makes the **anchor title** the single highest-leverage field (recruiters filter by title and skim title-plus-company first), and free government data (O*NET, ESCO, BLS) can ground title-lab's market research instead of ad hoc web search. Third, career-counseling science supports turning the career-strategist from a verdict machine into an experiment designer using Gati's PIC elimination model, prototype conversations, and evidence from other people rather than self-ratings, which correlate only about .29 with real performance. The plugin's packaging also has a cheap, concrete fix: five identical copies of `workspace-conventions.md` should become one canonical file with a validator script, a write hook and a native `claude plugin eval` suite. The report ends with a prioritized list of changes mapped to specific files.

## The field went downstream and left the identity layer empty

The reference point anyone will compare career-os against is **santifer/career-ops**, launched April 4 2026, which reached **73K+ stars and 13.8K forks** by mid-2026 and runs on Claude Code, Codex and several other CLIs through the open Agent Skill standard ([GitHub santifer/career-ops](https://github.com/santifer/career-ops); [santifer.io](https://santifer.io/career-ops-system)). It is a job-search pipeline: portal scanning across 45+ companies, an A-H evaluation report with a 1-5 score per posting, PDF/LaTeX CV tailoring, a tracker, interview prep with a STAR+Reflection story bank, outreach drafts and offer review. It frames candidates through **six fixed role archetypes** (AI Platform/LLMOps, Agentic Workflows, Technical AI PM and so on) and has a `titles` mode, but nothing resembling iterative, reaction-driven title discovery for people outside those archetypes ([santifer.io](https://santifer.io/career-ops-system)). Its data model is a `cv.md`, an optional `article-digest.md` of proof points and a `profile.yml`: exactly the artifacts career-os's workspace could produce. That makes career-ops complementary rather than competitive. Career-os can credibly position itself as the upstream layer that generates verified inputs for career-ops or any other tailoring tool.

The long tail of Claude Code skill packs validates the provenance idea while also showing where career-os lags. **r-hedayati/claude-resume-skills** tags every claim [V] verified, [S] self-reported or [?] unsourced, and rejects [?] claims outright; it also defends against hidden-text prompt injection and dismisses ATS-beating as "largely fictional" ([GitHub](https://github.com/r-hedayati/claude-resume-skills)). career-ops goes further at output time: it **blocks a PDF** whose numbers or facts appear in neither the CV nor the article digest unless the user overrides ([GitHub santifer/career-ops](https://github.com/santifer/career-ops)). Career-os's four-tag scheme (`[stated]`, `[source:]`, `[researched:]`, `[suggested → approved]`) is richer than [V]/[S]/[?], but nothing in the plugin enforces it; there is no `scripts/` directory, no hook and no eval. Competitors gate output; career-os asks nicely.

Commercial products split into job-search suites (Teal, Huntr, Careerflow, Simplify), interview tools, and a fast-growing **brag-doc category** (BragDoc, BragLog, BragBook, BragJournal, TrackToBrag) that logs wins continuously, sometimes straight from git commits, often on-device ([bragdoc.ai](https://www.bragdoc.ai/); [braglog.app](https://braglog.app/)). The only exploration tools, Kickresume Career Map and Google's US-only Career Dreamer, map a resume to fixed-taxonomy career paths with salary overlays and draft a "Career Identity Statement" ([Kickresume](https://www.kickresume.com/en/ai-career-map/); [Grow with Google](https://grow.google/career-dreamer)). None stress-test claims against evidence, and none iterate on titles. The brag-doc trend exposes a real gap: career-os's `portfolio-manifest` is a batch ingest, with no low-friction path to append a new EV item the week something happens.

User complaints line up neatly with career-os's design bets. The dominant grievances are generic AI voice ("spearheaded", "results-driven professional") and builders that **invent metrics and technologies** which then embarrass candidates in interviews; Reddit's stated preference is AI as a "resume assistant, not a resume ghostwriter" ([VisualCV](https://www.visualcv.com/blog/best-ai-resume-builders-reddit/)). Privacy is a selling point too: LinkedIn began training generative AI on profiles and resumes by default from November 3 2025 in the EU, UK, Canada and elsewhere ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/linkedin-set-to-expand-ai-training-on-user-profiles-heres-how-to-stop-it-using-your-personal-data)), and career-ops, BragLog and BragDoc all market local-first storage. Career-os keeps everything in a local markdown folder but never says so in its README. These complaint sources are mostly vendor-authored review sites summarizing Reddit, so treat them as directional.

## Labels alone leave half of outputs fabricated

The most important single source for career-os is a 2026 preprint on fabrication in multi-stage LLM hiring pipelines (resume improvement, then interview-question generation, then answer feedback). With no safeguards, **96.7% of outputs contained at least one unsupported claim, averaging 6.8 per output**. Prompt guardrails cut finding density by about 86% and eliminated invented identities and dropped qualifiers, yet **50% of outputs still contained fabrications**, mostly plausible additions. A structured human checkpoint cut density 59% and almost eliminated the capture of planted job-description "trap" requirements (47% to 2%), but reviewers caught only about **55% of qualifier drops and new claims** ([arXiv 2608.26171](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26171)). The study also showed **propagation**: a fabrication introduced while improving the resume became the factual premise of later interview questions and feedback. It is a single-author preprint with small samples, so the exact percentages are indicative, but the direction matches every practitioner complaint above.

The paper's fabrication taxonomy translates directly into validator rules. It names five categories: identity fabrications (invented names or credentials), **absence-list hits** (plausible skills the candidate explicitly lacks), **qualifier drops** (removing "basic" or "assisted"), altered claims (inflated scope, role or metrics), and new unsupported claims ([arXiv 2608.26171](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26171)). Career-os already captures half the needed inputs: EV items carry `My role`, `Result` and `Sources`, and ground truth captures anti-skills. What it lacks is an explicit **"does not have / not claimed" list** to grep against, a requirement that every outward-facing claim cite at least one EV ID, a check that numbers and titles in a draft actually appear in the cited EV note, and a review step that shows the user a claim-by-claim diff focused on qualifier drops and new claims, the two categories humans miss most. Because fabrications propagate, anything that draws on prior drafts (an interview story bank, a role-tailored intro) should read approved evidence directly, never earlier generated text.

Anthropic's own skill-authoring guidance points to how to build this. It advises **low-freedom deterministic scripts** for fragile, consistency-critical operations, a "run validator, fix errors, repeat" loop that "greatly improves output quality," and plan-validate-execute for high-stakes writes ([Anthropic: Skill authoring best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)). Career-os's frontmatter enums, ISO dates, EV-ID uniqueness, dangling references, untagged claims and changelog entries are textbook script territory. A W3C PROV-style mapping makes the tags machine-checkable without RDF: `[source: X]` is a `wasDerivedFrom` link to a document, `[suggested → approved]` is agent-generated then attributed to the user on approval ([W3C PROV-O](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/)), so adding optional `derived_from: [EV-###]` and `approved_on:` fields per claim would let a script walk every chain.

## Recruiters search the anchor title first, so ground it in real data

Title-lab's core design (searchable anchor plus expressive positioning title) is well supported by hiring-side evidence, and the evidence suggests sharpening it. LinkedIn Recruiter supports Boolean search in the **Job titles** filter ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/recruiter/answer/a415295)), and sourcing guides advise **OR-ing a cluster of related titles** rather than keyword search ([ZoomInfo/Pipeline](https://pipeline.zoominfo.com/recruiting/boolean-searches-for-recruiters)). The Ladders eye-tracking study found an average **7.4-second initial skim** that goes to current title and company, then previous title and company, then dates ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/eye-tracking-study-shows-recruiters-look-at-resumes-for-7-seconds/541582/)); it dates from 2018 and was vendor-run. Meanwhile the famous "75% of resumes rejected by ATS" figure traces to 2012 marketing by a defunct optimization company, and **92% of 25 recruiters** interviewed in 2025 said their systems do not auto-reject on keywords or formatting ([IT Brief](https://itbrief.co.uk/story/study-reveals-ats-rarely-auto-rejects-cvs-debunks-75-myth)). The practical upshot for title-lab: optimize for the recruiter's title filter and skim, output the OR-cluster of synonyms a recruiter would type, and drop "beat the bots" framing. Widely repeated claims that the LinkedIn headline carries "60% of search ranking weight" have no LinkedIn primary source and should not appear in skill guidance.

Title-lab currently researches each candidate by ad hoc WebSearch. A free, structured pipeline exists. **O*NET Web Services** offers a free keyed REST API (CC BY 4.0, attribution required) with keyword-to-occupation search, sample reported titles and related occupations across 900+ occupations ([O*NET Web Services](https://services.onetcenter.org/about); [O*NET reference](https://services.onetcenter.org/reference/)); it also exposes an **ESCO crosswalk** for non-US and multilingual users ([O*NET ESCO crosswalk](https://services.onetcenter.org/reference/online/crosswalk/esco)). **BLS OEWS** gives a compensation and employment baseline by SOC code, 25 queries a day without a key or 500 with a free one ([BLS Developers](https://www.bls.gov/developers/home.htm)). Paid or closed alternatives are not viable for an open plugin: Lightcast ended free self-serve access in April 2026 ([Lightcast API Access](https://lightcast.io/open-skills/access)), and Levels.fyi's terms prohibit scraping and AI training ([Levels.fyi Terms](https://www.levels.fyi/about/terms.html)). The catch is that government taxonomies collapse hybrid titles into broad SOC codes, so O*NET is strong for the anchor layer and adjacency, weak for the expressive layer, and its pay data is a floor, not a market rate for a design engineer. Live posting counts remain necessary for emerging titles, and the skill should degrade gracefully to web search when the user has registered no API key.

Title volatility makes runtime verification essential. **Forward Deployed Engineer** postings on Indeed went from 643 in April 2025 to 5,330 in April 2026 ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/forward-deployed-engineers-emerge-as-one-of-ais-fastest-growing-jobs/)), and US titles containing AI terms rose from 264 to 822 between Q1 2022 and Q1 2026, with 63% outside tech occupations ([Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-is-no-longer-just-a-tech-occupation-story/)), which means an "AI" prefix increasingly dilutes rather than signals. Design Engineer is a canonical title at design-led companies such as Vercel ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/blog/design-engineering-at-vercel)) but lacks reliable volume data, and Creative Technologist remains an agency and advertising umbrella ([Robert Half](https://www.roberthalf.com/us/en/insights/hiring-help/what-does-a-creative-technologist-really-do)). Title-lab's `title-patterns.md` already lists these as "emerging titles"; it should add which market each one anchors in.

Two bodies of theory refine how title-lab and positioning-studio choose. April Dunford's positioning method chooses the **market category last** and recommends positioning in an existing category by default, since category creation requires teaching the market new vocabulary; she also warns against "phantom competitors" that never actually appear in deals ([Dunford, Quickstart Guide](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning)). Mapped to careers (an inference; no study tests Dunford on job seekers), the anchor title is the existing category, an invented title is category creation and should be flagged high-risk unless the user already has inbound momentum, and "competitive alternatives" becomes "what would the hiring manager do if you did not exist": hire a frontend engineer plus a designer, use an agency, or ship without polish. The sociology of labor markets adds hard evidence on the "generalist discount": audiences devalue category-spanners unless the person shows **clear relevance to the specific job** or has strong reputation ([Leung, Berkeley IRLE](https://irle.berkeley.edu/publications/working-papers/an-idiosyncrasy-credit-or-a-generalist-discount-conditional-advantages-to-working-broadly-in-a-virtual-labor-market/)); founders who **specialize on one dimension** (function) while spanning another (industry) suffer less devaluation ([Organization Science 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v34y2023i2p557-588.html)); and the **order** of varied experiences shapes whether breadth reads as dilettante or renaissance ([Leung 2014, ASR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0003122413518638)). For career-os's users, that converts "the hybrid is the asset" into a more precise rule: be a legible specialist on one axis per audience, lead with the 2-3 most relevant proofs, and narrate the arc.

Finally, positioning-studio has no feedback loop from the market. LinkedIn's **Search Appearances** analytics report how often a member appears in search and aggregate searcher job titles and companies ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a553050/view-your-profile-search-appearances)), enough to run sequential, one-variable headline tests over 2-4 week windows. It is not a true A/B split and is confounded by time and activity, but comparing searcher titles to the positioning core's "For" field turns the wrong-fit stress test from opinion into data.

## Counseling science favors experiments over verdicts

Meta-analyses of career-choice interventions identify what makes them work. Brown and colleagues' review of 62 studies found five **critical ingredients**: written exercises, individualized interpretation and feedback, world-of-work information, modeling, and attention to building support ([Brown et al. 2003, JVB](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0001879102000520)); a 2017 replication of 57 studies added counselor support, values clarification and psychoeducation, with career decision self-efficacy showing the largest effect, d ≈ .45 ([Whiston et al. 2017](https://ohiostate.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effectiveness-of-career-choice-interventions-a-meta-analytic-repl/)). Career-os already covers written exercises, individualized feedback and values. It is thinnest on **world-of-work information** (the O*NET pipeline above fills this), **modeling** (stories of people who made similar hybrid moves; interest-radar's people items are a natural home) and **building support** (who in the user's network will back the move). Whether an AI agent can substitute for the "counselor support" ingredient is untested; Whiston's finding concerns human counselors.

The strategist's "filters first, then pull × proof" logic is close to the best-supported career decision model, Gati's **PIC** (Prescreening, In-depth exploration, Choice). PIC eliminates options sequentially by rank-ordered aspects, stops at a shortlist of about **7±2**, then explores in depth; people who used sequential elimination and chose a recommended occupation reported greater satisfaction **six years later** ([Gati & Asher](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264415740_Prescreening_In-Depth_Exploration_and_Choice_From_Decision_Theory_to_Career_Counseling_Practice)). The current agent applies dealbreakers as a flat pass/fail; PIC adds rank order, visible kill reasons per option, acceptable versus optimal ranges, and a sensitivity check ("relax this aspect and these options return").

Several decision-science findings argue for changing the strategist's output from "pick a target" to "design a test." Ibarra's study of career changers frames transitions as trying on **possible selves** through crafting experiments, shifting connections and sense-making ([Working Identity summary](https://careernlifedesign.com/working-identity/)); Stanford's Designing Your Life course reported fewer dysfunctional beliefs and more career options among students ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/06/spreading-life-design-thinking-universities)), though that is program reporting, not an RCT. People overestimate the intensity and duration of their feelings about outcomes, driven by focalism ([Wilson & Gilbert 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x)), which argues for debiasing prompts ("what else fills a typical Tuesday in that role?") and prototype conversations with people doing the work. Levitt's randomized coin-flip study found people undecided about a major change who were nudged to make it were **happier six months later** ([Levitt 2021, RES](https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/88/1/378/5834495)); it is one study of self-selected volunteers, so cite it cautiously when a user is stuck between staying and leaving. Premortems are useful for risk brainstorming, but the original "30%" result measured how many reasons people generated, not their accuracy ([Jason Collins](https://corporate.jcx.au/premortem)). Newport's career-capital argument ([Commoncog summary](https://commoncog.com/so-good-they-cant-ignore-you/)) is anecdote rather than research, but it supplies a sensible counterweight: an option with strong pull and no proof should be flagged, not recommended.

The weakest data in the workspace is self-rated skill. A meta-analysis of 55 studies found self-evaluations of ability correlate only **r = .29** with performance, and accuracy rose when people **expected verification** and made **explicit social comparisons** ([Mabe & West 1982](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232439390_Validity_of_self-evaluation_of_ability_A_review_and_meta-analysis)); a synthesis of 22 meta-analyses confirmed .29 and found ratings more accurate when domain-specific and task-concrete ([Zell & Krizan 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691613518075)). Ground-truth-interview's Expert/Competent/Learning ranking of "hard skills" is precisely the broad, unverified format that performs worst. Evidence from others performs better: the **Reflected Best Self** method collects specific "at your best" stories from 10-20 people and finds patterns ([Roberts et al. 2005, AMR](https://www.unm.edu/~plutgen/Best-self%20Feedback%20Process%20Heaphy%20et%20al%202005.pdf)). The name and kit are trademarked and sold by Michigan's Center for Positive Organizations ([CPO](https://positiveorgs.bus.umich.edu/cpo-tools/rbse/)), so career-os should implement a generic "others' best-self stories" request with attribution to the paper, not the kit.

For narrative depth, Savickas's **Career Construction Interview** uses five prompts (role models, favorite media, favorite story, motto, early recollections) to surface a life theme ([Savickas et al., My Career Story](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf)); gains in career adaptability held at three months, with no added benefit beyond three sessions ([IAFOR 2024](https://papers.iafor.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/bce2024/BCE2024_83395.pdf)). The evidence is small-sample but positive, and a life theme ("I want to move from X to Y by doing Z") gives hybrid users exactly the coherent narrative arc that the Leung "order of experiences" finding rewards. On instruments, the O*NET Interest Profiler (RIASEC) is the most agent-usable: a Tools Developer License permits modification with attribution, a non-endorsement notice and a validation requirement ([O*NET Tools License](https://www.onetcenter.org/license_tools.html)). VIA forbids redistributing items and scoring ([VIA FAQ](https://www.viacharacter.org/faq)), CliftonStrengths is paid, and the CAAS adaptability scale has no published license, so treat all three as user-supplied inputs only.

Two failure modes of LLM coaching need explicit guardrails. The ELEPHANT benchmark found LLMs **validate users on advice queries 72% of the time versus 22% for humans** and affirm whichever side of a conflict the user takes in 48% of cases ([ELEPHANT, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13995)), and models cave more readily to rebuttals that arrive as follow-up turns ([EMNLP Findings 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.1222.pdf)). The strategist's "Don't flatter" line is a start, but it needs a concrete rule: give the assessment first, and do not change an evidence-based judgment under pushback without new evidence. On bias, five LLMs including Claude advised **lower salary targets** for women and some minorities with otherwise identical profiles (for example $400K versus $280K for a Denver medical specialist) ([arXiv 2506.10491](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10491)), while resume-evaluation studies find the opposite direction, with neutral identifiers restoring parity ([arXiv 2505.17049](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17049)). Career-os captures comp floors and asks title-lab for "comp signal," so any compensation guidance should derive only from cited market data and the user's stated numbers, and demographic cues should be stripped from context passed to subagents.

## One conventions file and a native eval suite make quality testable

Career-os ships five byte-identical copies of `references/workspace-conventions.md` (107 lines each, same checksum today), one per skill. They will drift. Claude Code documents `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}` for referencing "resources shared between the plugin's skills" ([Claude Code: Skills](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/skills)), but Codex and the agentskills.io standard assume self-contained skill folders and keep references one level deep ([agentskills.io](https://agentskills.io/specification)). Since career-os targets both platforms, the safer pattern is one canonical source plus a sync script and a CI check that fails on drift. The same file should open with a table of contents, since Anthropic notes reference files over 100 lines can be partially read ([Anthropic best practices](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/agent-skills/best-practices)).

`claude plugin eval` (added in Claude Code v2.1.269) now provides a native harness: each case runs in an isolated session **with and without the plugin**, reporting the lift, using `regex`, `tool_used`, `tool_order`, `file_exists`, `llm` and `baseline` graders; `context.history_file` sets up mid-conversation states and `scaffold_script` creates fixture workspaces ([Test plugins with evals](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugin-evals)). There are no custom-code graders, so the documented pattern is to have the run execute the validator and write its output to a file that a regex grader checks. Eval runs also isolate personal memory and CLAUDE.md, and reports may publish to claude.ai unless `--no-publish` is passed, so the suite should use synthetic fixtures only. This makes the four risks above testable: triggering overlap between five similar skills, sycophantic caving mid-interview, fabrication against a thin evidence base with a trap-laden job description, and untagged claims in generated files.

## Conclusion

The research reframes career-os's main risk. Its weakness is not missing features; career-ops and the commercial suites have downstream features covered, and chasing them would dilute the one lane nobody else serves. The risk is that career-os's central promise, provenance, currently lives only in prose instructions, which the fabrication evidence shows cuts errors sharply but still leaves them in half of outputs. A validator, a hook, an absence list and an eval suite would turn provenance from a convention into a guarantee, and that guarantee is exactly what users complaining about invented metrics say they want and what downstream tools like career-ops need as input.

The second shift is epistemic. Career-os currently trusts the user's self-report and its own synthesis more than the evidence supports: self-ratings track performance weakly, LLM coaches validate too readily, and the job market's own vocabulary is measurable through O*NET, live postings and LinkedIn analytics. The highest-value upgrades therefore all move the same direction, toward evidence from outside the conversation: from other people, from the labor market, and from small real-world experiments whose results flow back into the workspace. For people whose work does not fit a title, that outside evidence is what turns "hybrid" from a generalist discount into a legible specialty.
